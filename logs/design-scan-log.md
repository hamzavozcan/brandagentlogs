# Design Scan Log

## 2026-10-01

### 1. Trends

1. **Waffle knit as the new layering base.** Waffle-knit long sleeves and thermals are replacing heavyweight cotton tees as the go-to layering piece in streetwear. The texture reads as elevated without loud graphics, breathes better than fleece, and photographs well because the knit pattern catches light. Multiple brands (Caracas Merch, True Classic, Etsy independents) are moving units here. Relevant because the brand doesn't currently sell any knit-based pieces — this is an open category.

2. **Architectural oversize replacing generic oversize.** The oversized silhouette is evolving: instead of just scaling up a pattern, brands are designing pieces with intentional 3D shapes using heavyweight fabrics (300–400 GSM) that hold their form. The shift is from "big because big" to structured drape. EME Studios' FW26 "ARCHIE" collection leans into this with boxy workwear jackets and structured wide-leg trousers that maintain shape. This matters for the brand because distressed pieces can look shapeless — a structured silhouette underneath the distressing would add contrast.

3. **Enzyme and bio-washed finishes overtaking traditional stone wash.** Manufacturers and brands are moving toward enzyme washing and pigment dyeing to get the aged, lived-in look while preserving fabric softness and structure. Stone wash is reading as dated next to these softer-hand finishes. The brand already uses acid wash and heavy wash — enzyme wash is a natural next technique to test on new pieces.

4. **Monthly cadence replacing fortnightly drops.** EME Studios publicly shifted from fortnightly drops to monthly releases in 2026, and 99Based now structures around seasonal capsules with WhatsApp early-access windows. The trend is toward fewer, more complete drops rather than constant small restocks. This affects how new products should be timed — launching a single new category per month with a proper content push, rather than trickling pieces out.

5. **Acid wash denim revival.** Acid wash is coming back hard across denim specifically (not just sweats/fleece), inspired by 1980s nostalgia. The brand already owns acid wash on its tracksuits — there's an opportunity to extend the technique into denim pieces before the trend peaks and commoditizes.

### 2. Design Directions

1. **Enzyme-washed waffle-knit henley, long sleeve.** Item: oversized henley in a heavy waffle knit (280+ GSM). Finish: enzyme wash for a soft, broken-in hand; light distressing at the cuffs and button placket only. Colorway: faded sky blue (ties to signature color) with tonal stitching. Why it fits: introduces a knit-based piece to the lineup, uses the brand's signature color, and the waffle texture adds visual interest without graphics — sits naturally next to the distressed hoodies and tracksuits. Estimated retail: $65–$75.

2. **Acid-washed denim cargo pants.** Item: relaxed-fit cargo pant in 12 oz denim. Finish: acid wash with enzyme softening; subtle distressing at pocket edges and knee. Colorway: washed black with sky blue topstitching on cargo pocket flaps. Why it fits: extends the acid wash language from the tracksuits into a new product category (denim bottoms), the cargo silhouette is trending but the acid wash treatment keeps it on-brand rather than generic gorpcore. The sky blue topstitching detail is a quiet brand signature. Estimated retail: $85–$95.

3. **Two-layer long sleeve with contrast wash.** Item: heavyweight cotton long sleeve (320 GSM) with extended cuffs that roll back to reveal an inner layer. Finish: outer layer in heavy garment wash (charcoal); inner cuff and hem band in enzyme-washed sky blue. Distressing along the shoulders and collar. Colorway: washed charcoal / sky blue reveal. Why it fits: applies the brand's signature layering-and-reveal concept to a long sleeve format — a product type the brand doesn't currently sell. The reveal is subtle (cuffs and hem only), not a full lining like the tracksuit, so it reads as a new idea rather than a remix. Estimated retail: $60–$70.

4. **Pigment-dyed structured vest with hidden print lining.** Item: boxy cut vest in heavyweight french terry (360 GSM). Finish: heavy pigment dye for an uneven, sun-faded look; distressing at the armholes and hem. Lining: tonal animal print (muted, not snow leopard — think a washed-out python or crocodile texture in grey-on-grey) visible when unzipped. Colorway: pigment-dyed slate with grey tonal lining. Why it fits: vests are an approved direction, the pigment-dye finish is distinct from the acid wash already in the lineup, and the tonal animal print lining continues the reveal DNA without repeating snow leopard. The structured boxy cut follows the architectural oversize trend. Estimated retail: $75–$85.

### 3. Competitor Spotlight

**EME Studios — FW26 "ARCHIE" collection and international expansion.**

What they did: EME Studios, a Spanish streetwear brand, dropped its Fall/Winter 2026 collection "ARCHIE" that reimagines varsity and collegiate style through a modern streetwear lens — chunky rugby-striped sweaters, archive-inspired knits, boxy hoodies, structured workwear jackets, and wide-leg trousers in denim, corduroy, and wool blends. Simultaneously, they opened their first permanent store outside Spain (London) and ran a temporary pop-up in New York, and launched their first womenswear line earlier in the year.

What's working: EME is executing a textbook scale-up — slowing their drop cadence to monthly while increasing the completeness of each collection. The "ARCHIE" collection works because it has a clear reference point (varsity/collegiate) reinterpreted through their lens, rather than trying to do everything. Their accessories (leather belts, striped scarves, hobo bags) round out the collection and increase average order value. The international retail expansion gives them physical touchpoints that build brand credibility.

Transferable or not: The retail expansion and womenswear diversification are beyond this brand's current stage. But two things are directly transferable: (1) the "single reference point per collection" strategy — each drop organized around one clear mood or era rather than a grab-bag of pieces, and (2) adding accessories as collection-completers. A distressed belt, a washed-out scarf, or a pigment-dyed bag would round out a drop without requiring a new garment category.

### 4. New Brands to Watch

1. **Hidden Season** (@hidden.season) — ~45K followers. Los Angeles-based, founded by Jordan Killgore in 2024. Takes essentials (sweats, polos, jackets, pants) and gives them unexpected details: khakis with rips and repair patches, nylon track jackets in plaid patterns, polo shirts with multicolored buttons. The ethos ("no comment, just clothes") is anti-hype, letting the garments do the talking. Relevant because the brand operates in a similar "take a familiar piece and twist it" lane, and the repair-patch detail on distressed garments is worth studying.

2. **Organic Garmentz** (@organicgarmentz) — ~19K followers. New York-based, known for playful construction details: pants with adjustable waist ties, a wallet designed to look like a folded NYC traffic ticket, and the "Tie Hat" (half head scarf, half baseball cap). The brand proves you can be fun and inventive with garment construction without going loud with graphics. Relevant for the construction-innovation approach — the brand could borrow the mindset of "one weird functional detail per piece" rather than relying solely on wash and distress for differentiation.

3. **Mertra** — recently launched, rapidly growing. Strong visual proposition blending urban minimalism with sports references through technical pieces. Worth watching as they represent the newer wave of brands that launch with a fully formed visual identity from day one, using tight content loops on TikTok and Instagram to build an audience before the product even ships. The brand's technical-meets-urban lane is adjacent but not identical — useful for studying launch strategy and content pacing.
