# Design Scan Log

---

## 2026-10-02

### 1. Trends

1. **Distressed knitwear with mixed-texture construction.** Open-weave knits, slip-stitch patterns, and mixed-gauge yarns within a single garment are gaining traction for fall/winter 2026. Designers are combining cable + rib, jacquard + solid knit, and contrasting yarn weights to create depth without relying on graphics. The distressed edge and faded wash treatments that already dominate cotton fleece are now being applied to knitwear — uneven color effects, worn-in textures, and raw hems on sweaters and cardigans.

2. **Mesh jerseys as a streetwear layering staple.** Mesh tops and sport-inspired jerseys have moved from niche skate culture into mainstream streetwear layering. The styling is mesh over a long-sleeve tee or under an open jacket — reveal without exposure. Bold graphics on breathable mesh fabrics create a layered dimensional look that photographs well for social content. This is a new vehicle for the reveal/conceal concept.

3. **Vintage-washed wide-leg cargo pants with stacking.** Wide-leg cargos with garment-dyed faded finishes are one of the most searched categories in men's streetwear right now. The silhouette pools over chunky footwear for a stacking effect. Washed black, olive, and sand colorways dominate. The trend is moving toward more refined construction — still oversized, but with intentional tailoring rather than shapeless volume.

4. **Boxer-waist detailing on sweatpants and bottoms.** An exposed waistband treatment that mimics boxer shorts is emerging as a design detail on streetwear bottoms. It adds a subtle design element without going loud, and works well with the oversized/layered aesthetic.

5. **Monthly drop cadence replacing rapid-fire releases.** EME Studios publicly shifted from fortnightly drops to monthly releases in 2026, citing "slower communication" and more considered design. Other mid-tier brands are following. The signal: smaller brands can compete on quality and narrative rather than volume, and audiences are responding to fewer, more intentional releases.

### 2. Design Directions

1. **Distressed open-weave knit sweater — sky blue / washed slate.** A heavyweight open-weave crew neck with mixed-gauge knit panels, raw hems, and a pigment-washed finish. Sky blue body with slate grey contrast stitching. The open weave creates a natural reveal effect (skin or a layer underneath shows through) without needing a separate lining. Fits the lineup because it extends the distressed/washed DNA into a new product category the brand doesn't currently sell, and sky blue is the signature color. Estimated retail: $75-$90.

2. **Vintage-washed wide-leg cargo pants — acid-washed black / charcoal.** Wide-leg cut with utilitarian cargo pockets, acid-washed black finish with charcoal fade, and distressed hem detailing. The acid wash treatment matches the brand's existing finish language. Stacking silhouette pairs naturally with the existing hoodies and tops. This is a bottoms category the brand doesn't currently cover. Estimated retail: $80-$95.

3. **Washed heavyweight rugby shirt — navy / sky blue stripe.** An oversized rugby shirt in a heavyweight washed cotton, with a navy body, sky blue collar and two-tone stripe, and the cursive logo embroidered small on the chest. Pigment-washed for a lived-in feel. Distressed rib collar and cuffs. Rugby shirts are re-emerging in the collegiate-meets-street space (EME Studios leaned into rugby stripes for FW26), and this gives the brand a structured knit top that sits between a tee and a hoodie. Estimated retail: $65-$80.

4. **Mesh basketball jersey with washed finish — smoke grey / sky blue.** An oversized mesh jersey in a garment-washed smoke grey with sky blue trim and cursive logo heat-pressed on the front. Designed as a layering piece over long-sleeve tees. The mesh format is trending as a streetwear layering vehicle, and applying the brand's washed finish to it keeps it in-lane while opening a completely new product type. Estimated retail: $60-$70.

### 3. Competitor Spotlight

**Cold Culture Worldwide** is doing notable work right now. Their March 2026 drop introduced a Biker Zip Hoodie line (black, navy, heather grey) that borrows motorcycle culture construction — reinforced panel stitching, PU leather details, and hardware accents — and applies it to a streetwear zip hoodie silhouette. The construction is the story: it reads as more "built" than "sewn," with visible seam engineering and patch/screen-print graphics. They also dropped a "Timeless Knitwear Blue" piece alongside distressed defense hoodies, showing they're expanding from their core fleece category into knit. Cold Culture's approach of taking a familiar silhouette (zip hoodie) and elevating it through construction detailing rather than graphic overload is transferable — the principle of "better built, not louder" applies to any category. The specific biker/moto direction is not a fit for this brand's washed/distressed lane, but the construction-as-design-feature approach is worth studying for future pieces.

### 4. New Brands to Watch

1. **Hidden Season** (@hidden.season) — ~45K followers. Founded 2024 by Jordan Killgore in Los Angeles. Builds around vintage-influenced workwear and denim with minimal branding. Standout moves: remixing khakis with rips and repair patches usually reserved for vintage jeans, nylon track jackets covered in plaid patterns typically found on flannel shirts, and polo shirts with extra multicolored buttons on the placket. The crossover of workwear construction with unexpected pattern placement has a similar "familiar silhouette, unexpected detail" energy to what this brand does with layering reveals. Worth watching for how they handle fabric treatments.

2. **Zic** (@zic) — small following, growing fast. Founded 2023 by Isaac Allen (21 years old) in New York City. Entirely silhouette-driven — focused on cut, shape, and feel rather than branding or graphics. Complex named them in their "10 brands to know before they blow up in 2026" list. The brand proves that shape alone can carry a collection at streetwear price points, which is a useful reference point for any brand considering how far to push silhouette before needing graphics. Not a direct aesthetic match, but the discipline of silhouette-first design is relevant.

3. **Taller de Rafa** (@tallerderafa) — growing NYC presence, Bronx-based. Founded by sibling duo Genesis and Rafael Valverde during the pandemic. Started as a screen-printing operation, now a full streetwear imprint. Their signature is automotive/mechanic culture translated into apparel: hardware shaped like nuts and bolts on hoodies and jeans, handbags shaped like paint cans. Landed a Nike Air Max Goadome Low collab for FW26 and received a CFDA spotlight. The hardware-as-detail approach is interesting — this brand could study how Taller de Rafa uses physical hardware elements as a brand signature, similar to how the cursive logo and distressing function as signatures here.
